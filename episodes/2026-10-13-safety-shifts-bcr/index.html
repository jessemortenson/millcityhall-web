Safety Shifts pt 2: Behavioral Crisis Response Faces Big Proposed Changes

October 13, 2026

Minneapolis safety programs are facing big shifts, putting key strategies at risk. In part two, we focus on Behavioral Crisis Response (BCR). BCR was built out by a local mental health care provider, Canopy Roots, that received a very positive evaluation this year. However, city staff now recommend changing vendors, just a couple months before the contract is slated to expire. Staff also want to change the scope of the program. Why change now? does this put a successful safety program at risk of gaps in service, or even compromise its focus and excellence? A lot of possible change is swirling around BCR at this moment, making it critical that we speak up.

What is the Behavioral Crisis Response Model?

In the US, the pioneer was a mental health clinic in Eugene, Oregon - White Bird Clinic - which started a program called Crisis Assistance Helping Out On The Streets (CAHOOTS). CAHOOTS operated successfully for decades, earning the support of community members as well as other first-responders. CAHOOTS teams respond to behavioral emergencies with staff trained to do that work. It's a nonviolent, caring response. The program diverted something like 5-8% of emergency calls away from police response.

In mostly 12-hour shifts, CAHOOTS teams made up of a medic and a crisis worker respond to urgent medical or psychological crises. Employees do not carry guns or wear uniforms; instead, they wear casual hoodies and drive vans with a dove painted on the side. (source).

CAHOOTS is meaningfully distinct from other late 20th-century police policy changes around mental health care, such as "Crisis Intervention Teams" or "co-response." Both of those involve training police officers and/or sending a social worker or other mental health care provider along with a police response. The CAHOOTS model does not send police to mental health calls, instead emphasizing a health care and peer-support response.

More information on CAHOOTS:

  • What is CAHOOTS - White Bird Clinic
  • High Country News overview
    • "In 2017, such teams answered 17% of the Eugene Police Department’s overall call volume. This has saved the city, on average, $8.5 million each year from 2014-2017, according to the White Bird Clinic."
    • "Staff employ 'unconditional positive regard,' a phrase from psychology that means complete support and acceptance for the people they encounter, and the organization is run as a 'consensus collective,' rather than a hierarchy. Every employee’s voice carries equal weight."
  • 2025: the program was halted due to a financial crisis at White Bird clinic
  • In 2020, annual budget was $2.1 million

Denver, CO

In Denver, the Support Team Assisted Response - STAR - program was started in 2020. Denver is an interesting comparison point for the Twin Cities, because its metro is somewhat similar in population, with 2.9 million population compared to the Twin Cities urban population of 2.7 million (per Wikipedia). Of course, Minneapolis is just half of the Twin Cities metro, with a 430k population, while Denver's city-limits population is 715k.

Program budgets are also similar: the 2026 budget for STAR is $6.3m, which is a little more than the $5.9m proposed for the pending 2027 BCR contract in Minneapolis.

The Denver program seems to have made a positive impact, but is not fully funded. It does not operate 24/7. Looking at STAR, I would say we're doing a better job in Minneapolis. STAR is successful, but underfunded and showing some of resulting weaknesses in terms of operations and outcomes.

Minneapolis Behavioral Crisis Response (BCR)

BCR now responds 24/7 and citywide, with 40 responders handling 10k annual calls. This service is provided by Canopy Roots.

Proposal to Change Vendor & Program Scope

City staff presented a proposed contract to hire Community Bridges to replace Canopy Roots as program provider, and to switch the brand/scope of the program from Behavioral Crisis Response to "Mobile Crisis Response."

The 10/7 Committee meeting moved this proposal on to the City Council with a recommendation to reject the proposed contract with Community Bridges.
