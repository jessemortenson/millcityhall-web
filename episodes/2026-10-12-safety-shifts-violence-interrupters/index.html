Safety Shifts pt 1: Violence Interrupters Face Big Budget Cut

October 12, 2026

Minneapolis safety programs are facing big shifts, putting key strategies at risk. In this part one, we tackle a severe budget cut proposed by the Mayor for the violence interrupters program. What is violence interruption? We talk about the theory and practice, including its role in a very successful violence prevention strategy in Baltimore. Recent Minneapolis history has been troubled. But we argue that the city cannot afford to let this violence prevention strategy wither.

Part 2, coming soon, will focus on Behavioral Crisis Response.

Johns Hopkins Violence Prevention Best Practices

Below quoted from John Hopkins

  • Community gun violence is concentrated in poor, segregated, and disinvested neighborhoods with few economic opportunities. These neighborhoods have suffered from a legacy of racist and discriminatory public policies which create the conditions for violence. Consequently, Black Americans are disproportionately impacted by community gun violence; they suffer from a gun homicide rate 14 times higher than their white peers.
  • A small number of people within these disinvested neighborhoods drive most of the violence. They are often caught in cycles of victimization, trauma, and retaliation, are disconnected from work and school, and lack the community supports to help them heal.
  • reduce gun violence in the most impacted neighborhoods through outreach by credible messengers who work with individuals involved in gun violence. These programs are most effective when cities and states invest in comprehensive intervention and prevention efforts that engage a wide range of stakeholders and community leaders.
  • Strategies:
    • Violence Interrupter Programs (such as Cure Violence): credible memberso f the community trained to interrupt
      • The Center for Gun Violence Solutions partnered with the city of Baltimore to evaluate Safe Streets, a violence interrupter program. Findings from 2007-2022 support the hypothesis that Safe Streets Baltimore reduced homicides and nonfatal shootings over the many years the program operated in some of Baltimore’s most under-resourced neighborhoods. The average reductions ranged from 16% to 23% with larger reductions in homicides during the first four years of the longer running sites.1
    • Focused Deterrence / Group Violence Intervention: identify chronic violent offenders, present them with combo carrot/stick
      • An analysis of 24 focused deterrence programs found that these strategies led to an overall statistically significant reduction in firearm violence. The most successful of these programs have reduced violent crime in cities by an average of 30% and improved relations between law enforcement officers and the neighborhoods they serve.2
    • Cognitive Behavioral Interventions with Wraparound Supports: dentify participants highly impacted by violence and provide cognitive behavioral interventions paired with wraparound social services, such as job placement, life coaching,
      • Evaluations of programs that use cognitive behavioral interventions with wraparound supports like READI Chicago and Advance Peace show large reductions in arrests for violence and violent victimization of study participants.3,4 These promising early results indicate that identifying individuals at high risk and supporting them with job training and cognitive behavioral therapy can improve their safety, the safety of others, and promote economic opportunity.
    • Violence Reduction Councils / Homicide Review Commissions: council examines individual homicides and shootings to identify system gaps and missed opportunities for intervention
      • The homicide review commission in Milwaukee was associated with a significant and sustained 52% reduction in homicides. A Department of Justice evaluation found shooting review boards to be an effective way to reduce gun violence by building trust between criminal justice stakeholders and the community
    • Changing Environmental Risk Factors for Violence: large numbers of vacant buildings and blighted lots, a high density of alcohol outlets, and inadequate street lighting, can reduce gun violence in a community.
      • Funding for programs that clean and rehabilitate blighted and abandoned property are associated with both decreases in gun violence of up to 39% over one year and improved community health.

Media coverage of Minneapolis Violence Interruption Program Issues

  • 10/5/26 Fox9: Minneapolis violence prevention funding in question after critical audit
    • "An internal city audit found Minneapolis failed to properly oversee millions of dollars in violence prevention contracts, increasing the risk of fraud and waste."
      • Previous FOX 9 data requests revealed that one violence prevention consultant billed over $270,000 for personnel, rent, and food, yet the city never produced a single receipt for the company. Despite those red flags, the city council kept funding the group for years.
    • FREY: "The findings are not entirely surprising really at all," Frey said. "We have known the shortcomings of the whole framework for quite some time."
      • "When you are wrong, you admit it, and you try to do better," Frey added. "And that's what we are trying to do here."
    • Mayor Jacob Frey is proposing to slash the Violence Interrupter budget in half due to compliance tracking issues, a move facing pushback from some city council members.
  • 9/27/26 Strib: City audit warns of fraud and favoritism in Minneapolis violence prevention programs
    • Five years into the initiative, the city still lacks consistent site visits, adequate invoice records and safeguards against political interference.
    • oversight of more than 100 contracts has been so lax that it’s increased the risk of fraud while enabling favoritism in contracting, a recent city audit found.
    • Since 2023, the city has spent more than $36 million on alternatives to police through five main programs in which community groups are hired to help reduce street violence
    • Neighborhood Safety Department Director Amanda Harrington said 140 site visits were done from the spring of 2025 to 2026, but they weren’t documented the way auditors wanted
    • Last year, Commissioner of Community Safety Todd Barnette publicly accused some progressive council members of playing politics with the programs and supporting “efforts that would benefit them or their favorite nonprofit.”
    • Harrington said that, since she took the helm of her department in May 2025, she’s seen a couple of instances where contractors reached out to the mayor or council members about contract terms being negotiated or invoices not being processed quickly enough. Even though contracts clearly say all communication should go through contract managers, “that doesn’t stop them.”
    • Thomson said his firm spent “well over six figures on this litigation pro bono” to make sure the program was being properly run, and two years later, little has been done. The remedy would be for someone to bring another lawsuit.
    • This year, $5.5 million is budgeted for the violence interrupter program, and Mayor Jacob Frey has proposed cutting the program by $2.6 million next year, calling it a** **“noble program filled with committed and talented community leaders, but clear tracking has not demonstrated the level of results taxpayers deserve.”
    • Council Member Jason Chavez and Council Vice President Jamal Osman recently vowed to restore their funding.
  • 9/5/26 KSTP: Questions remain on steps to close oversight gaps in Minneapolis violence prevention programs
    • Thomson: "My goodness, two years ago with the settlement, they knew there was a problem,” he said. “Yet two years later, they’re saying, ‘Well, we intend to fix these problems.’ That’s not an adequate response.”

    • At issue was a proposed $643,000 contract for Salem Inc., a nonprofit associated with the Rev. Jerry McAfee’s church, New Salem Missionary Baptist Church. McAfee also runs the public safety outreach nonprofit 21 Days of Peace.
