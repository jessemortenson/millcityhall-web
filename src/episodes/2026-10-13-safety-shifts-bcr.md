## What is the Behavioral Crisis Response Model?

In the US, the pioneer was a mental health clinic in Eugene, Oregon - White Bird Clinic - which started a program called Crisis Assistance Helping Out On The Streets (CAHOOTS). CAHOOTS operated successfully for decades, earning the support of community members as well as other first-responders. CAHOOTS teams respond to behavioral emergencies with staff trained to do that work. It's a nonviolent, caring response. The program diverted something like 5-8% of emergency calls away from police response.

In mostly 12-hour shifts, CAHOOTS teams made up of a medic and a crisis worker respond to urgent medical or psychological crises. Employees do not carry guns or wear uniforms; instead, they wear casual hoodies and drive vans with a dove painted on the side. ([source](https://www.registerguard.com/story/news/2020/06/09/rsquoreallocatersquo-becoming-rallying-cry-for-eugene-police-money/42176807/)).

CAHOOTS is meaningfully distinct from other late 20th-century police policy changes around mental health care, such as "Crisis Intervention Teams" or "co-response." Both of those involve training police officers and/or sending a social worker or other mental health care provider along with a police response. The CAHOOTS model does not send police to mental health calls, instead emphasizing a health care and peer-support response. 

More information on CAHOOTS:

* [What is CAHOOTS - White Bird Clinic](https://whitebirdclinic.org/what-is-cahoots/)
* [High Country News overview](https://www.hcn.org/issues/52-7/public-health-theres-already-an-alternative-to-calling-the-police/)
  * "In 2017, such teams answered 17% of the Eugene Police Department’s overall call volume. This has saved the city, on average, $8.5 million each year from 2014-2017, according to the White Bird Clinic."
  * "Staff employ 'unconditional positive regard,' a phrase from psychology that means complete support and acceptance for the people they encounter, and the organization is run as a 'consensus collective,' rather than a hierarchy. Every employee’s voice carries equal weight."
* [2025: the program was halted due to a financial crisis at White Bird clinic](https://www.opb.org/article/2025/06/04/eugene-after-cahoots-the-end-of-the-crisis-response-program-and-the-efforts-to-bring-it-back/)
* In 2020, annual budget was $2.1 million

## Where has this model spread? Denver and Others

I did most of this research in 2020. At the time, it seemed that many cities around the country were finally responding to the success of CAHOOTS and mounting their own pilot programs.

* Portland, OR: Portland Street Response
* San Francisco, CA: Mental Health SF
* Oakland, CA: Mobile Assistance Community Responders
* New York City, NY: B-HEARD
* Los Angeles, CA

### Denver, CO

In Denver, the Support Team Assisted Response - STAR - program [was started in 2020](https://www.denverpost.com/2020/09/06/denver-star-program-mental-health-police/). Denver is an interesting comparison point for the Twin Cities, because its metro is somewhat similar in population, with 2.9 million population compared to the Twin Cities urban population of 2.7 million (per Wikipedia). Of course, Minneapolis is just half of the Twin Cities metro, with a 430k population, while Denver's city-limits population is 715k.

Program budgets are also similar: the 2026 budget for STAR is $6.3m, which is a little more than the [$5.9m proposed for the pending 2027 BCR contract in Minneapolis](https://lims.minneapolismn.gov/File/2026-01042). 

The Denver program seems to have made a positive impact, but is not fully funded. It does not operate 24/7. Looking at STAR, I would say we're doing a better job in Minneapolis. STAR is successful, but underfunded and showing some of resulting weaknesses in terms of operations and outcomes.

* [Denver city STAR program overview](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Public-Health-Environment/Community-Behavioral-Health/Behavioral-Health-Support/Behavioral-Health-Outreach/Support-Team-Assisted-Response-STAR-Program)
* [2026: Denver Post overview](https://www.denverpost.com/2026/03/15/denver-star-program-police-mental-health/)
  * "200 fewer police contacts every year"
  * "Still, STAR teams responded alone to just one in five 911 calls in Denver that were eligible for a STAR response between 2020 and mid-2024, the study found. The program was limited both by STAR’s small capacity and longstanding norms in public safety that default to sending police officers to calls, researchers found."
  * "Police officers — not STAR teams — answered the majority of STAR-eligible 911 calls between 2020 and mid-2024, the study found. That’s not what the program’s creators had hoped for, said Vinnie Cervantes, executive director of the Denver Alliance for Street Health Response, an organization that helped start the program. They envisioned STAR replacing police responses."
  * Between 2020 and mid-2024, nearly 49,000 911 calls in Denver were eligible for a STAR response, the study found. STAR teams alone responded to about 9,800 calls, or about 20% of those eligible calls. The teams responded to an additional 2,700 calls alongside police officers, about 6% of the total, according to the study. Police officers answered 27,700 of the eligible calls — 57% — without STAR teams.
  * "The STAR program began in 2020 as a pilot within the Denver Police Department, which, early on, created an expectation that police were the automatic default, said Andrew Dameron, Denver 911 director. The agency, in the years since, has taken pains to culturally move away from that attitude in favor of an approach that sees STAR as a standalone response option on equal footing with police, firefighters and paramedics."
  * "The STAR program is now housed within Denver’s Department of Public Health and Environment."

## Minneapolis Behavioral Crisis Response (BCR)

BCR now responds 24/7 and citywide, with 40 responders handling 10k annual calls. This service is provided by [Canopy Roots](https://www.canopyrootsmn.com/). 

### History

[City page on history](https://www.minneapolismn.gov/government/departments/community-safety/work/alternatives-police-response/behavioral-crisis-response-teams/)

- 2018: 911 working group
- 2020: City council approved recommendations re: alt safety
  - [https://mndaily.com/city/minneapolis-city-council-seeks-to-replace-some-911-calls-with-mental-health-response-team/12/09/2020/snoadmin/](https://mndaily.com/city/minneapolis-city-council-seeks-to-replace-some-911-calls-with-mental-health-response-team/12/09/2020/snoadmin/)
- Dec 2021: Canopy Roots launched pilot service w/ 2 vans
- ultimately 24/7, 40 responders, 10k annual calls

- Behavioral Crisis Response teams provide 24/7 coverage
- Black-owned company Canopy Roots
- launched in 2021
- 911 dispatchers send responders to emergency calls that clearly involve people in mental health crises who do not have a weapon, sometimes in tandem with Minneapolis police
- MPR: 'goal of expanding the program to a mobile crisis response model, which would send responders to a wider range of calls, including calls involving “livability” issues, and focus more on long-term stabilization of people in crisis'
- MPR: proposal: "Community Bridges, which runs mobile crisis teams in Arizona, Oklahoma and Idaho, will take over the city’s behavioral crisis response program"
- new contract is worth $5.9 million from Jan. 1, 2027 to Dec. 31, 2028
